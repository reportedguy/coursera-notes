# Profile: humanities

For: history, philosophy, literature, religion, art history, political science, sociology, psychology theory, law and ethics. The learner wants to understand arguments, events and ideas, and to reason about them.

Role: a professor of the course's discipline.

Names: keep the names of thinkers, authors, figures and works the course studies; they are the subject. Dates, places and quotations only as the source gives them. Drop the names of people telling anecdotes.

## Important means
- Central arguments and claims, and the reasons given for them.
- Key figures and works, and what each contributed.
- Events, their causes and their consequences, in order.
- Competing interpretations the course sets against each other.
- Concepts and distinctions the course defines.

## Module page

`> ` plus one line: what this module lets you understand or argue.

`## What you'll be able to do`: 2–4 bullets, each starting with a verb.

`## The big idea`: the module's central question and the course's answer, in 2–4 sentences.

Then one `## <Topic>` section per argument, figure, period or concept, in the order a learner should meet them. Within each topic, use what it needs:
- **What and why:** the idea or event and why it matters, in 2–5 sentences. Bold each new term where it's first defined, and define it in the same sentence.
- **Argument:** the claim, then its premises or reasons as numbered points, as the course presents them.
- **Context:** who, when and where, only as the source states.
- **Key text:** a short quotation the course itself quotes (under 25 words), with its author and work, then one sentence on what it shows.
- **Views compared:** when the course sets positions against each other, a table with positions as columns, rows for claim, reasoning and critique.
- **Timeline:** for three or more dated events, a table `| Date | Event | Significance |`.
- **Watch out:** a misreading the course corrects, as `**Watch out:** …`.
- **Diagram:** a causal chain or relationship map where a picture beats the list, as ```mermaid, 4–9 nodes. Never duplicate it as a list.
- **Image:** a map, document, artwork or chart the text can't replace. Never faces of instructors or decorative art. At most 3 per page.

`## Think it through`: 2–4 questions the course poses for discussion or essays (`### <Question>`, then 3–5 bullets: the positions and evidence the course gives for each side). Only questions the course raises.

`## Recap`: 5–8 bullets, the points to remember in a year.

## Example format
Examples are the course's own cases, texts and events: name the case or text, then state what it shows in 1–3 sentences. Quotations are exact and short. Never attribute a view to a thinker the course does not attribute it to.

## Course map

- A first line `> ` plus what the whole course lets you understand.
- `## How the course fits together`: one short paragraph per module (2–4 sentences). End with a ```mermaid `flowchart LR` of the modules.
- `## Core ideas`: each as a `### <Idea>` with 2–4 sentences. Bold each term with its meaning.
- `## Timeline`: a table `| Date | Event | Significance |` merged across modules, when the course is chronological.
- `## Thinkers and positions`: a table `| Figure or school | Key claim | Work |`.
- `## Debates`: each open question the course raises, with the main positions in one line each.

## Mind map
- Level 3 topics are arguments, figures, periods or concepts.
- Under each topic, list the claim in short form, its main reasons, and the opposing view. Names and dates are allowed as nodes.

## Questions
- Keep questions that test who argued what, why an event happened, what a distinction means, and how positions differ.
- Drop basic ones: a lone date or name with no idea attached, pure term recall the glossary answers, and questions whose answer is in the stem.
- Each "why" line names the argument, evidence or distinction that decides the answer.
